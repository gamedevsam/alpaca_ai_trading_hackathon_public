This page is the write-up for **MANDATE**, our entry in the Alpaca AI Trading Agents
Hackathon. It trades a **paper** account — simulated money — and has never been pointed at
a live one.

## The claim

Most trading agents ask you to trust that the model behaved. This one is built so you
never have to: **an LLM proposes, deterministic code disposes, and every order carries a
record of the rules it was measured against — including the ones it failed.**

The strategy is not in the source. It is a **mandate**: a paragraph of plain English a
human wrote, versioned like a document, that says what this desk is allowed to want. Change
the mandate and the desk changes behaviour. Change nothing else.

Here is the mandate the desk is running right now, in full:

> You run a small premium-selling desk on a $100,000 paper account. Sell cash-secured puts
> — out of the money, expiring this Friday, at strikes you would be content to own the name
> at — on SPY, QQQ, IWM, or on a large, liquid stock where a creator you follow has made a
> bullish call you find credible. Prefer the strike with |delta| ≤ 0.20 that still pays at
> least $0.30 of premium. Never carry more than two open contracts per underlying. Treat a
> creator's call as a claim to test, not an instruction: say in one sentence why it survives
> your own judgment, or decline it. If implied volatility is unusually low, or the index is
> within 1% of its 52-week high with a scheduled macro event before expiry, propose nothing
> and say why. State, per proposal, the condition under which you would be wrong by Friday's
> close.

## One cycle, end to end

A **trade cycle** is one pass of the desk. It runs on a schedule during market hours, and
nobody is watching it.

1. **Read the world.** Account, positions and resting orders from Alpaca; the live option
   chain for the mandate's underlyings; and any **signals** — investment claims extracted
   from the YouTube channels the owner follows (see below).
2. **Reconcile the book first.** Every order the desk believes is still resting is
   re-checked against the broker before any collateral maths runs, so the desk never
   reasons from a stale position.
3. **Propose.** One LLM call, one prompt, one persona: the mandate, the account, the
   candidate contracts, and the creators' open claims go in; a small set of candidate
   trades with a written rationale and a falsifiable "how I would be wrong" condition
   comes back.
4. **Dispose.** Every candidate is scored against **21 named ceilings** in plain,
   non-AI code. A candidate that clears all of them becomes a *proposal*. One that fails
   any of them is *discarded* — never quietly resized to fit.
5. **Approve.** Either a human approves it, or — if the owner has deliberately set the
   desk's autonomy gate to `fully_autonomous` — the desk approves its own paper proposal.
   Either way the ceilings are re-run at execution time against the account as it is *now*.
6. **Submit.** The order goes to Alpaca through **Alpaca's own CLI**, dry-run first, then
   for real. The exact command and the broker's exact reply are stored.
7. **Record.** Every stage writes a timestamped event onto the action. That record is the
   [proof bundle](/docs/hackathon/proof-bundle).

## The AI logic

**One agent, one prompt, one call per cycle.** There is no chain of agents debating each
other, because every extra hop is another place a hallucination can enter with nothing to
catch it.

What the model is asked to do is deliberately narrow: read a mandate and a chain, pick
contracts, and *argue for them*. What it is never asked to do is decide whether a trade is
safe. It cannot see the ceilings, cannot negotiate them, and cannot tell whether a proposal
it made was accepted or thrown away.

Two properties fall out of that split:

- **The model can be wrong without being dangerous.** A bad proposal is a discarded row
  with a reason attached, not a bad order.
- **A silent model is not a silent desk.** If inference returns nothing — no key, a bad
  route, an empty completion — that is treated as a hard failure and recorded. The desk
  does not fall back to trading on its own.

Reasoning runs on Claude Haiku 4.5 through OpenRouter; the bulk transcript work runs on a
cheaper model, because reading video is a volume problem, not a judgment one.

## The risk gates

These are the 21 ceilings. Eleven apply to any order; ten more apply to options. They are
pure functions with no I/O, which is why they can be — and are — exhaustively unit-tested.

| Gate | What it refuses |
| --- | --- |
| `kill_switch` | Anything at all, when the owner has killed the desk |
| `order_request` | A malformed order (missing limit price, both qty and notional, …) |
| `symbol_tradable` | A symbol the broker does not list as tradable |
| `symbol_not_denied` | Anything on the owner's deny-list |
| `symbol_allowed` | Anything off the owner's allow-list, when one is set |
| `sufficient_holdings` | Selling more shares than are actually held |
| `buying_power` | A buy the account cannot fund |
| `max_notional_per_order` | A single order above the owner's dollar cap |
| `max_position_pct` | A trade that would push one name past its weight cap |
| `max_orders_per_day` | The runaway case — more orders in a day than the owner allows |
| `max_daily_notional` | Too much total money moved in one day |
| `options_enabled` | Every option order, when options are switched off |
| `defined_risk_floor` | **Any naked short option, ever** — a short call needs the shares, a short put needs the cash, and the cash figure is the broker's own options buying power, not our estimate |
| `max_contracts_per_order` | An oversized single option order |
| `max_contracts_per_underlying` | Stacking too many contracts on one name |
| `short_call_coverage_pct` | Signing away more of a holding's upside than allowed |
| `dte_bounds` | An expiry outside the allowed window |
| `strike_otm` | A strike that is not out of the money |
| `strike_vs_cost_basis` | A covered call struck below what the shares cost |
| `delta_ceiling` | A contract more likely to be assigned than allowed |
| `earnings_proximity` | Writing through an earnings date inside the blackout |

Three properties matter more than the list itself:

- **They fail closed.** If a configured check cannot be evaluated — a missing greek, an
  unavailable quote — it fails. A safety check is never skipped because its input was
  inconvenient.
- **They run twice.** Once when the proposal is made, and again at execution against the
  live account. An approval authorises the checks to run; it does not skip them.
- **Their verdicts are kept.** All 21 results, pass and fail, with the numbers that
  produced them, are stored on the action forever. That is what makes "it obeyed" checkable
  rather than assertable.

Above all of them sits the **kill switch**, which is the owner's alone and outranks the
autonomy gate. And in code, the client refuses to construct a live-trading connection at
all: `live` is not a configuration this build accepts.

## Alpaca infrastructure

The desk uses **both** of Alpaca's agent surfaces, for different jobs:

- **The Alpaca CLI (`alpaca` v0.0.14) is the live execution path.** Every order is placed by
  shelling out to `alpaca order submit` — dry-run first to validate the request, then for
  real. The CLI is installed into the deployed image by a dedicated Go build stage. The
  submitted command string and the broker's JSON reply are both persisted verbatim.
- **Alpaca's own MCP server runs as a private sidecar.** Thirty-one of its read tools are
  re-exported to the owner's AI assistant under an `alpaca_` prefix, so he can ask questions
  of the brokerage in chat. **No write tool is proxied** — the sidecar's trading toolset is
  switched off, and it has no public port.

Market data (chains, quotes, greeks, the clock) comes from Alpaca's REST API through a typed
client, because that is the hottest path in the cycle and it should have exactly one
implementation.

## What the creators add

The desk follows up to three YouTube channels the owner already watches. Each new video's
transcript is read once, and any falsifiable investment claim in it is extracted as a
**signal**: the verbatim quote, the ticker, the direction, a horizon, and a condition that
will make it true or false — then filed in the same prediction ledger the rest of the
platform uses, so the creator gets scored on it later whether or not the desk agreed.

Today that is 18 signals across three channels. The desk reads the open ones each cycle and
must say, in one sentence, why it acted on a claim or declined it. A real decline, from the
record:

> **The Patient Investor**, on QQQ: *"We're like making lower highs over the last three
> times on QQQ… I think we will most likely retest 630 to 640."*
>
> **The desk:** *"Bearish call on QQQ — desk only sells puts, and selling a put here would
> trade directly against his thesis; also his market-timing record is unproven (0 resolved
> claims), so there is no graded edge to lean on."*

That is the whole point of the feature: not "the AI listens to influencers", but **a
creator's opinion enters the system as a claim that can be graded, and the desk has to
justify itself against it either way.**

## What the record shows so far

The honest headline first: **this is a tiny sample and nothing has been held to expiry
yet.** No hit rate is being claimed, and the account's equity is still exactly its
$100,000 starting balance.

What has actually happened, read off the desk on 2026-09-03:

- **16 actions** created by the desk. **11 were discarded by a ceiling** before reaching the
  broker. **1 reached the broker** — a QQQ cash-secured put, submitted through the Alpaca
  CLI with no human click — and rested until it was cancelled.
- **4 were refused by Alpaca itself** for insufficient options buying power. Those refusals
  are the most useful thing that happened all week: they exposed that we were counting
  short-put collateral per-name while the broker counts it account-wide, and that a
  cancelled order was still being treated as a live collateral pledge. Both are fixed; the
  desk now takes the broker's own options-buying-power figure as authoritative and re-checks
  every resting order at the top of each cycle.
- **18 signals** extracted from 3 channels, each one filed as a dated, falsifiable claim.

We would rather show a small, legible record than a large, unfalsifiable one.

## What was built this week, and what wasn't

Stated plainly, because it should not be discovered later: **the platform underneath this
desk is not hackathon work.** The portfolio system, the versioned entity store, the
prediction ledger and the AI-agent layer have been in development since June. The Alpaca
module was started before the hackathon and stopped at *proposal* — it could reason and
compute, and it could not place an order.

Built during the hackathon week:

- The Alpaca **CLI** execution path, and Alpaca's **MCP server** as a private sidecar.
- The **autonomy gate** and unattended, scheduled trade cycles.
- **Real-market candidate generation** off the live option chain.
- The **followed-channel → transcript → signal → graded claim** pipeline, and the desk
  reasoning over those claims.
- The **desk view** — the mandate, its proposals, their proof bundles and the scorecards, in
  one screen.
- The two correctness fixes the live account forced on us: account-wide collateral, and
  reconciling resting orders.
